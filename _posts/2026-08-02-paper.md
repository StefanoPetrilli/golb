## Introduction

Using synthetic data for computer vision has attracted a lot of research efforts as it already allows to reduce reliance on expensive and manual data generation and annotation. Several approaches have been tried to generate synthetic data, videogames, diffusion models ... .

One of the approaches used to generate synthetic data is the one proposed by Dwibedi [https://arxiv.org/pdf/1708.01642]. More recently, Zlotnikov et al. [arXiv:2606.09495] introduce ContextShift, a controlled benchmark that isolates object–background compatibility as an independent variable using an NPMI-based co-occurrence score. They find a consistent failure signature under context shift — increased false negatives and reduced total predictions, with false positives remaining stable — which they attribute to a breakdown in detection candidate formation rather than reduced confidence in existing candidates. In a proof-of-concept mitigation experiment, they fine-tune a YOLO detector on COCO train2017 augmented with a small (~9%) slice of manipulated images per condition, and find that every augmented variant — including compatibility-driven background substitution — outperforms an unaugmented baseline on both clean and manipulated evaluation sets.
However, ContextShift's mitigation experiment fixes augmentation volume at a single value per manipulation type; it establishes that context-decoupled augmentation helps, but does not vary volume independently of background plausibility. It therefore cannot speak to whether a larger quantity of implausible-context synthetic data can substitute for a smaller quantity of plausible or photorealistic data — which is the specific tradeoff practitioners face when deciding how to allocate a fixed data-generation budget.

By removing the constraint that the objects have to be pasted on a related background, generating the images becomes cheaper. For instance, this would open the door to procedurally generated backgrounds and, when using a real background, it would not require an additional manual or machine selection. In turn, this would allow for a far bigger training corpus. The central question then becomes wheter this bigger corpus of training data would offset the advantage of background coherency.

This paper reframes this tradeoff as a measurable quantity: the exchange rate between cheap, context-agnostic copy-paste images and costly, context-plausible ones. Instead of asking which condition wins at equal volume, we ask: how many implausible-context images are needed to match the improvement delivered by a single plausible-context image?

To answer this, we first replicate the finding of Dvornik et al. on a modern, state-of-the-art detector, establishing the plausible-versus-implausible gap as a reference point. We then fine-tune the same pretrained detector under a fixed base dataset and training protocol while varying two factors independently: background plausibility (graded from matched natural backgrounds to mismatched natural and procedural backgrounds) and augmentation volume (swept over an order of magnitude). Plotting detection performance against volume for each plausibility level yields a family of scaling curves; the horizontal distance between two curves at a fixed performance level is the exchange rate between those data types.

This quantity turns the plausibility-versus-volume debate into a concrete budgeting rule. If generating one plausible image costs as much as k cheap images, then cheap volume is the better investment exactly when the measured exchange rate exceeds k. Every outcome is informative: an exchange rate near one implies plausibility is irrelevant and only volume matters; a finite rate larger than one yields a practical cost-benefit rule for synthetic data generation; and saturation of the cheap-data curve below plausible-data performance — an infinite exchange rate — would mean no amount of cheap data substitutes for context, qualifying the conclusions of large-scale copy-paste studies.

## Methodology

- 1 detector (YOLO-family); a second architecture deferred to Possible improvements
- 3 seeds per detector
- plausibility arms: matched natural / mismatched natural / procedural
- volume grid: log-spaced per arm (e.g., 1k, 2k, 5k, 10k, 20k, 50k)
- metrics: AP@0.5 + ContextShift behavioral metrics (ΔFN/img, Δpred/img)
- analysis: fit scaling curves per arm, read off exchange rate at fixed performance levels, report with bootstrap CIs; compare against cost ratio k

## Test setup

**Dataset.** Base training set: COCO train2017 (118k images, 80 classes), using the instance segmentation masks as the object bank — every augmented image is built by cutting 1–3 COCO instances and pasting them onto a new background, keeping the original boxes/masks as labels. Evaluation: the same 990-image held-out split of COCO val2017 used by ContextShift, plus the ContextShift manipulated evaluation sets, for direct comparability.

**Plausibility arms.** Three background sources, one per arm:

- *Matched natural*: Places365 scenes selected with the precomputed NPMI object–scene compatibility matrix from ContextShift (COCO classes × Places365 categories, built with YOLO-World); paste only on scenes with NPMI in the top quartile.
- *Mismatched natural*: same Places365 pool, bottom-quartile NPMI — natural photos, wrong context. This is the Dvornik-style random-placement condition.
- *Procedural*: randomly generated non-photorealistic backgrounds (random textures, gradients, geometric patterns) in the spirit of Tobin et al.'s domain randomization.

Identical object instances, scales, positions, and blending (Poisson blending + distractor objects, as in Dwibedi et al.) across arms, so background plausibility is the only varying factor.

**Volume grid.** Per arm: 1k, 2k, 5k, 10k, 20k, 50k augmented images, each added to the full 118k COCO base. Plus the 0-augmentation baseline. That is 3 × 6 + 1 = 19 training configurations per seed.

**Detector and training.** YOLO26m (Ultralytics) — the model ContextShift uses for its mitigation experiment, and one of five architectures ContextShift shows exhibits the same context-suppression pattern — initialized from COCO-pretrained weights. Fine-tuning protocol fixed across all configurations: 10 epochs, batch 64, 640×640 input, default Ultralytics hyperparameters (SGD, lr 0.01, cosine schedule, standard mosaic/flips disabled for the augmented copies so the manipulation is the only augmentation), 3 random seeds. Total: 19 × 3 seeds = 57 runs.

**Pretraining control.** The COCO-pretrained init means the model already carries COCO's context prior, so the sweep measures the incremental value of augmentation when *fine-tuning* — which is the practitioner scenario. Moreover, the released `yolo26m.pt` checkpoint was itself pretrained with copy-paste augmentation (copy_paste = 0.304, mosaic ≈ 1.0 per the Ultralytics YOLO26 training recipe), i.e. its prior is already partially context-decoupled; the measured exchange rate is therefore expected to be biased toward 1 and should be read as a lower bound on the value of plausibility. All fine-tuning runs set copy_paste = 0 so the experimental manipulation is the only copy-paste in training.

**Controlling for the pretrained prior.** The released `yolo26m.pt` was pretrained with copy_paste = 0.304 (verified in the checkpoint's embedded train_args), i.e. its prior is already partially context-decoupled. This biases the measured exchange rate toward 1, so a rate clearly above 1 still stands — as a lower bound. The ambiguous outcome is a rate ≈ 1, where genuine irrelevance of plausibility is indistinguishable from an already-decoupled prior; only then, run a from-scratch check: YOLO26s from random init, base vs. base+20k matched vs. base+20k mismatched, 1 seed (3 runs), ~245 epochs, MuSGD, recipe as published but copy_paste = 0.

**Replication checkpoint.** Before the sweep, replicate Dvornik et al. on YOLO26m: VOC'12 trainval with its segmentation masks, few-shot splits (10/50/100 examples per class), matched vs. random placement at equal volume. Critically, this arm is initialized from the ImageNet-pretrained backbone, *not* COCO-pretrained weights — COCO pretraining already covers all 20 VOC classes, which would make the few-shot setting meaningless. This anchors the plausible–implausible gap on a modern detector before measuring its exchange rate.

**Metrics and readout.** AP@0.5 as the headline metric; ΔFN/img and Δpred/img from ContextShift to detect the prediction-suppression signature. Per arm, fit AP against log(volume), then read the horizontal distance between curves at fixed AP levels: that distance, in images, is the exchange rate. Bootstrap over seeds for confidence intervals; decision rule against the generation-cost ratio k as described above.

## Possible improvements

- **Diffusion-generated backgrounds.** A fourth plausibility arm: text-to-image generated scenes (prompted with scene descriptions only, never the object class), no compatibility filtering. Sits between natural and procedural on the realism axis and is the arm most practitioners would actually reach for today; dropped from the initial sweep to keep the run count within budget.
- **Second architecture.** Re-run the sweep with RF-DETR-base, whose pretraining pipeline (LW-DETR lineage: scale jitter, random crop, hflip, random erasing — no copy-paste) provides a clean-prior cross-check: agreement between the two detectors' exchange rates would rule out the pretrained prior as the driver of any result, including rates ≈ 1, and would establish that the exchange rate is not YOLO-specific. ContextShift's finding that the suppression pattern is consistent across five architectures suggests it will hold; measuring it is still open.
