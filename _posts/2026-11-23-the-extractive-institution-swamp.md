I just finished reading *Why Nations Fail*, the book containing the ideas that earned Acemoglu et al.
a Nobel Prize in Economics. This is my synthesis of it.

Before I can state the thesis of the book concisely, I need to introduce two concepts: inclusive
and extractive institutions.

Inclusive institutions are regulations and public organizations that distribute value and
opportunity to a broad share of the population. They can be both political (universal suffrage,
freedom of speech, rule of law) and economic (protection of private property, a justice system
that enforces contracts).

Extractive institutions, in contrast, are regulations and public organizations that distribute
opportunity and wealth only to a minority of the population.

What Acemoglu and Robinson try to convey through the hundreds of examples and case studies in the
book is that once a country finds itself with extractive institutions, a negative spiral is
triggered. Whoever controls these institutions has disproportionately more power and wealth than
everyone else, and this elite has little incentive to make decisions that benefit the vast
majority. In fact, all else being equal, the elite is also scared of change.

I am paraphrasing Acemoglu here, but what I infer over and over from the book is that the elite
understands that a crowd that is uneducated, has no infrastructure, awful media and no technology
is easier to control than one that is educated, well connected, informed and technologically
advanced.

This results in a vicious cycle where the elite does everything in its power to block the rise of
any inclusive institution, while capturing and extracting as much value as it can.

An example the book returns to over and over is how European monarchs tried to stop the
development of railways and factories. They understood that if people could move faster, or lived
in high density cities, it would be easier for them to organize and protest against the monarchs.

When holding power brings so much wealth, many people compete for it. Sometimes, as in many of the
African examples, this results in a never ending spiral of civil wars and military coups.

At some point in time, every state was dominated by extractive institutions. The US, Japan,
Germany: no one is excluded. Yet these countries somehow managed to climb out of the swamp, thanks to
what Acemoglu et al. call the contingency of history.

In Western Europe, serfdom started to collapse only thanks to the specific demographic conditions that
arose after the Black Death: as 1/3 of the population died in the epidemic and the demand for workers
did not decrease as much, lords had to compete for workers and started offering better working
conditions.

In England, the crowd started eroding the absolute power of the monarchy thanks to the opening of
the Atlantic trade routes in the 1600s. This progressively created a new, broad coalition that
dismantled the power of the monarchy piece by piece, culminating in the Glorious Revolution, which
eventually led to the first democracy in the world.

Yet, the more time you spend in the swamp
of extractive institutions, the harder it is to get out. Also, not all swamps are created equal:
some start, or end up, in a much worse position than others.

Personally, I find this theory very elegant, and I believe it correlates quite strongly with
reality. If tomorrow it were proven to be causal, I would be extremely happy, because it would give
us more incentive to strive for a society that, from my POV, would be a better one to live in.

On the other hand, I am also aware that the wealth of nations is far too complex to be explained by
a single general theory... even by Acemoglu et al.'s own admission. Time has also not been kind to
this book: many of the contemporary case studies it presents have since reversed and been proven
wrong, [like Nogales (Sonora) and Nogales (Arizona)](https://www.econlib.org/library/columns/y2025/salinasleonnogales.html). 

IMHO, their theory, like any other theory we can formalize, can only explain a small part of reality,
not its full complexity.
